I have two master's degrees. I've sat exams, written theses, and taught myself new subjects from scratch more times than I can count. None of it prepared me for the night I was left alone in a hospital bed with a newborn I didn't know how to hold.

## The first night

My son was born by C-section in the Rotunda. Later that day my husband was asked to go home, and suddenly it was just the two of us: me and a tiny person I was entirely responsible for.

I had a catheter in and no feeling in my legs. I couldn't lift him out of the cot, so every feed meant pressing the call bell and waiting for a midwife to lift him onto me, sometimes with him crying near me for up to 10 minutes while I waited for someone to help. I didn't know if I was holding him correctly. I didn't know how to get him to latch. I didn't know how much he should be eating, for how long, or how often, or whether hunger was even why he was crying.

I remember lying there in the middle of the night, close to tears, genuinely afraid that I could hurt him without realising it.

That was the moment I understood that nobody hands you a manual.
{: .pullquote}

## What I thought the information would look like

Before he arrived, I assumed the guidance would come the way good instructions usually do: one step at a time, in the order you need it. Short and clear, something like:

- Before you leave hospital, book the lactation consultant.
- Here's what the first few feeds at home should look like.
- These are the signs of infection to watch for.

Instead, we came home with a bag of leaflets and the HSE's *My Child* book. It's a genuinely good resource, but it runs to about 200 pages, and I was trying to search it at 2am, one-handed, while recovering from major surgery.

## Even with every advantage

I want to be honest about this part, because it matters. I was a private patient under a consultant obstetrician. I stayed in the Lillie Suite, the midwives were wonderful, and I had access to private lactation consultants. By most measures I had every support available.

It was still overwhelming.

The problem wasn't a lack of information. It was the amount of it, and the way it reached me: scattered across booklets, websites and conversations, often contradictory, and arriving while I was healing from a C-section, surviving on broken sleep, and quietly working out who I was now that I was someone's mother.

## Just the two of us

Neither my husband nor I has family in Ireland. There was no mam down the road to call in, and no sister to text at 3am to ask "is this normal?"

When we got home to Kildare, it was the two of us, a newborn, and a search bar.

I know we're far from the only ones. A lot of parents in Ireland are raising babies a long way from the people who would normally show them how, and for all of them the gap between the hospital and home feels even wider.

## If you're the partner reading this

My husband was in it with me the whole way. He took the baby so I could sleep during the day, researched every question I couldn't face, and kept asking the most useful question there is: *what do you need right now?*

He'll be telling his side of the story in his own post. For now, if you're the partner of a new mum, the most helpful things are often the simplest. Take the baby so she can rest. Be the one who reads the leaflet and pulls out the three things that matter today. And ask her what she needs, rather than waiting to be told.

## Why I'm writing this

Becoming a mother in Ireland was harder than it needed to be. That wasn't because the information doesn't exist; it's because it isn't brought together in one place, in the order you need it, in a form you can take in when you're exhausted.

My husband and I want to use our experience to say something simple to other new mums and dads: if you're feeling overwhelmed, you're not alone, and it isn't because you're doing it wrong.
