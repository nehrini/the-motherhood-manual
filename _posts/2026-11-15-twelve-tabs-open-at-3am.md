These are real searches from my phone, typed one-handed in the dark with a newborn asleep on my chest:

- *Is my baby breathing normally?*
- *Is my baby supposed to eat so little?*
- *Is the grunting noise in his sleep a bad sign?*
- *Am I a failure as a mother for not knowing how to hold and latch my baby?*

The last one wasn't really a search. It was the question underneath all the others.

## The tabs

If you'd looked at my phone in those first weeks, you'd have found some version of all of these open at once:

- The HSE website and *mychild.ie*
- The Rotunda's antenatal class notes
- Cuidiú and La Leche League
- Mumsnet and Reddit threads, some of them years old and from other countries
- Google, endlessly
- AI chat tools, including Claude
- WhatsApp messages from friends with children in other countries

Every one of them had something useful in it. None of them had everything. And very few of them agreed with each other.

## Three midwives, three answers

My son struggled to latch from the start. Over the first couple of days I asked for help, and three different midwives gave me three different answers.

One told me to give him formula straight away. One told me I was holding him wrong, and that was why he wasn't latching. One told me to only offer the breast, as often as I could.

Each of them was kind, and each of them was confident. So I did all three, at different times, and none of it felt right. I ended up engorged, in pain, pumping, and in tears.

Then the midwives weighed him. He had lost 12% of his birth weight.

I can't fully describe how that felt. I felt like I was failing him, like I was already hurting his health when he was only days old, and that it was my fault because I couldn't feed my own baby. The advice kept coming, more formula, more conflicting breastfeeding tips, and it all blurred together.

In the end I stopped trying to follow everyone and trusted my instincts. I pumped and gave him my expressed milk in a bottle, because at least then I could *see* how much he was eating. It wasn't the plan I'd imagined. But it was the first time in days I felt like I knew what was going on.

That was the moment I understood the problem wasn't a lack of advice.

## The things I didn't know I didn't know

Some of what overwhelmed me was medical. A surprising amount of it was simply not knowing how the system works in Ireland, especially as neither my husband nor I grew up here.

**What a public health nurse is.** I found out what a PHN was when she rang me to arrange her first visit. Nobody had explained it before then. I'd have loved to know in advance that a nurse would come to our home in the first few days, who she was, and what I could ask her.

**That there's no paediatrician by default.** My husband and I both grew up in countries where babies are looked after by paediatricians. Here, your baby's first port of call is your GP. So when our son developed congestion at five weeks, we genuinely didn't know whether the GP was the right person to see.

**That children's hospitals are separate.** Our GP advised us to take him to Crumlin to be checked. That's when we realised that not every hospital has a paediatric A&E. Children's emergency care in Dublin happens at specific children's hospitals, not at the nearest adult hospital. Finding that out while worrying about your baby is not ideal.

**That advice from abroad doesn't always fit.** Our friends overseas meant well. Most of them told us to see a paediatrician, and many had a lactation consultant come to them on the ward as standard. Some had given birth in private maternity hospitals, which isn't how maternity care works here; even private care in Ireland happens inside the big maternity hospitals. Their advice was right for where they lived. It just wasn't right for Ireland.

## Before he even arrived

The information overload didn't start at the birth. During my pregnancy I developed gestational diabetes. That meant a strict diet, regular exercise, testing my blood sugar four times a day for weeks, and extra growth scans.

That was its own crash course, with its own set of leaflets, apps and websites. Looking back, it was good practice for what came next.

## If you're the partner reading this

My husband was searching just as hard as I was, just differently. When I was diagnosed with gestational diabetes, he went digging into everything around it, including related conditions like preeclampsia, so that he'd know what to watch for. After our son was born, he used AI tools to research my questions, held the baby so I could sleep during the day, and kept asking what I needed.

If you're a partner, one of the most useful things you can do is be the filter. Read the leaflets and the websites so she doesn't have to, and bring her the three things that matter today, not the thirty that might matter one day.

## What actually helped

Honestly? What helped us most was getting AI to do the job no single source was doing. We'd ask it to summarise what different sources said about a question, and to point out where they contradicted each other, so we could read it all in one place and decide for ourselves.

We're both comfortable with technology, so we leaned on that. But it shouldn't take a tech background to feel informed about your own baby.

## What I wish someone had told me

None of these are medical tips. They're the things I wish someone had sat down and said to me before we brought him home:

- **Feeling lost doesn't make you a bad mother.** Not knowing how to hold or latch your baby doesn't mean you're failing. It means you're new.
- **Conflicting advice isn't proof you're doing it wrong.** When three people tell you three different things, it's the information that's broken, not you.
- **Your instincts count.** You're the one watching your baby every hour. What you notice matters, and it's okay to say so.
- **Asking for help again is not failing.** Ask again, ask someone else, ask at 3am. Nobody is keeping score.
- **You're allowed to grieve the plan.** If feeding, the birth or the first days didn't go the way you imagined, that's a real loss and it's okay to feel it.
- **It's okay not to feel like yourself yet.** You're healing and becoming someone new at the same time. That takes longer than a few weeks.

None of this would have changed the advice I was given. But it would have changed how I felt about myself while I was trying to follow it.
