I never really made a feeding plan. I just assumed I would breastfeed. It's meant to be the most natural thing in the world, so I thought it would come naturally to us too.

It didn't.

## What nobody prepared me for

The antenatal classes and the advice I read while pregnant were all about how to breastfeed well: how to position the baby, how to get a good latch, what a good feed looks like.

All of that was useful. But nobody really talked about the alternatives. Nobody explained what exclusive pumping means for your body and your day. Nobody talked about how combination feeding works when you have no idea how much your baby is actually taking from you. The plan was breastfeeding, and there wasn't much of a plan B.

## Chained to the pump

After we left the hospital, I pumped every two hours to try to build my supply. Add it up, and it was hours of every day.

I'd pump, wash and sterilise, feed my son his bottle, and then it would almost be time to pump again. I felt chained to the machine. And every time I fed him from a bottle, I had this quiet feeling that this wasn't how it was supposed to go. This wasn't the picture I'd had in my head.

## My complicated feelings about formula

In the hospital, formula had been pushed as the answer to his weight loss, again and again. Somewhere in that, I developed an aversion to it. When we got home, I tried to avoid it at all costs.

When he hit his growth spurts, we did combination feed. But that brought its own confusion. If I didn't know how much he was getting from me, how was I supposed to know how big a top-up he needed? It felt like trying to solve a maths problem with half the numbers missing, at 3am, on no sleep.

## The visit that explained a lot

When the private lactation consultant came to our home, she noticed something nobody else had mentioned: our son had a tongue tie.

She told us it can run in families. That's when I found out I have one too, one that has never been treated. She explained what it meant for his feeding, and talked us through what a release would involve.

After weeks of wondering what I was doing wrong, it was a huge relief to hear there might be a reason it had been so hard. I wish we'd known sooner.

## The hardest judge

People were kind. Honestly, everyone around me was supportive of whatever I chose to do.

The person judging me hardest was me.
{: .pullquote}

There wasn't one comment or moment that stuck. It was a constant hum of confusion and doubt in the background: what had he eaten, when, and was it enough?

## Living by the numbers

Even after I managed to get him to latch, the panic didn't fully go away. With a bottle, at least I could see the millilitres. With a breastfeed, I didn't know how long was long enough, or how much he'd actually had.

What finally eased our minds was seeing him gaining weight steadily at his six-week check.

But even then, there was a question that stumped us every time: how many wet and dirty nappies has he had? We were both changing him, and neither of us had kept count. We'd look at each other, and guess.

## How we tracked it all

We tried a few baby apps. Most felt generic, and many of the features we actually wanted were behind a paywall, so we gave up on them.

In the end, we kept it simple: a chat with Claude for each of us, and sometimes a notebook. For basic tracking, that was enough. The problem was never the tool. It was remembering to write things down when you're half-asleep with a baby in your arms.

## If you're the partner reading this

My husband takes a stretch of the night with a bottle, and he still does. It means I get one slightly longer stretch of sleep. I honestly think that saved my sanity.

If you're a partner and your baby takes a bottle, even sometimes, one night feed can be the most loving thing you do all week. And if your baby is exclusively breastfed, there's still plenty you can do, like the burping, the changing, the settling, and bringing her water and something to eat.

## What I wish someone had told me

Not medical tips, just the things I needed to hear:

- **However you feed your baby, it's a real decision and it's yours.** It doesn't need to be defended to anyone.
- **Pumping is feeding.** Bottle feeding is feeding. Combination feeding is feeding. None of them are second place.
- **The kindest people in the world can't silence the voice in your own head.** Be as gentle with yourself as they are with you.
- **Finding out there's a reason it's hard can feel like a weight lifting.** It was never a lack of effort.
- **Not knowing the exact number doesn't mean you're failing.** It means you're human and you're tired.

People will always have opinions, and they'll share them. "Breast is best." "All mine were on formula and they turned out fine." They might even mean well. But how you feed your child can still feel deeply personal, because you had a way you wanted to do it. If it's not going the way you planned, it's okay to grieve that, and it's okay to choose what works for you and your baby.

If you're crying over a pump or a bottle at 3am, I've been there. You're doing better than you think.
