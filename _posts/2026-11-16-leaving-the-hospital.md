For five days in the Rotunda, all I wanted was to go home.

I wanted my own bed, my own shower, my own walls. But my son's weight had dropped, and they wouldn't let us leave until it started to climb again. Every weigh-in felt like an exam I was failing.

When the numbers finally turned and they said we could go, I was so relieved. It took me a few hours to realise that I was also terrified.

## What going home looked like

On discharge day, a midwife checked that the car seat was fitted properly. I was given a prescription for my medication. And there were leaflets. A lot of leaflets.

That was it.

We were pumping and bottle-feeding expressed milk by then, and it seemed to be working. But I had no real plan for home. I was relying on AI to work out how many millilitres he should be taking at each feed. We ordered our own pump before we left, because it was the one thing that seemed to help. The rest was guesswork.

## The drive home

I remember every bump in the road between Dublin and Kildare.

Our son slept the whole way. My husband drove more carefully than I have ever seen anyone drive, wincing at every pothole on my behalf. I sat beside the car seat, one hand on my scar, watching him breathe.

When we walked in our front door, I felt a little bit like myself again for the first time in days. Then my husband and I looked at each other and said, almost at the same time:

*Now what?*
{: .pullquote}

## The first night

In hospital, help was a button away. At home, it was just us.

The difference that first night was the anxiety. I was exhausted and I had to sleep, but I woke every time he startled or made a sound. My husband sat up watching over both of us. We didn't know what was normal, what wasn't, or at what point you're supposed to call someone. And we didn't really know who that someone was.

It was the longest night of my life, and nothing actually went wrong.

## Who do you call?

When something worried us in those first days, we called our family and friends, all of them in other countries.

We knew about the two-week and six-week GP checks, because the hospital had told us to book them in. Beyond that, we didn't know who we were allowed to call. Was this a question for the hospital? The GP? Someone else? We didn't want to waste anyone's time, and we didn't want to miss something important. So mostly we worried, and searched, and rang people who loved us but were thousands of kilometres away.

## The public health nurse

Then the public health nurse came, and she was wonderful.

She weighed him and measured his length, and plotted it on his growth chart. She checked my wound. She gave us good, kind advice. She also gave us more booklets: one about playing with your baby, one about vaccines, and the *My Child* book.

I was grateful for every one of them. But I remember sitting with the new pile on top of the old pile from the hospital, trying to work out feeding amounts and nappy counts and which booklet said what. Every source was helpful on its own. Together, they were a second job.

## Recovering while caring

What nobody quite prepares you for is looking after a newborn while you're recovering from major surgery.

I couldn't twist to lift him, so I relied on my husband for almost everything. I stayed in our bedroom rather than face the stairs. Some days I felt completely useless.

I was so tired I couldn't remember which side he'd fed from last, or how many millilitres he'd had that day. My husband and I were each logging feeds in our own Claude chats, but at 3am, half-asleep with a baby in your arms, remembering to type in the numbers is the first thing to go.

## Being asked how *I* was

Understandably, almost all the attention was on the baby. Was he feeding? Was he gaining? Was he okay?

People did ask about me too. And when they did, when someone asked how I was really doing and then just listened, it helped more than I can say. Being seen as a person, not just as the baby's feeding system, made the hard days feel a bit lighter.

## When he went back to work

My husband had two weeks of paternity leave and had saved up his holidays to add to it. It still wasn't enough time.

The first day I was alone with our son, he cluster-fed all day and wouldn't be put down. I was completely overwhelmed. Worse was the day I lost track entirely: when he'd last eaten, how much he'd had, whether his nappies were okay. I had no idea, and no one to ask in the room.

## Finding more help

A friend who'd had similar struggles with breastfeeding told me about booking a private lactation consultant. I wasn't well enough to get to the HSE primary care centre for the public lactation group, so I looked up how to book a consultant for a home visit.

What struck me was that I only found out because a friend happened to mention it. It wasn't in any of the booklets I'd been given, or if it was, I never found it.

## If you're the partner reading this

For those first weeks, my husband was my hands, my legs and often my memory. He lifted, carried, drove, watched and tracked, and somehow still asked me how I was.

If you're a partner, the first nights at home may be harder than the hospital. You might feel like the one who has to stay calm and in control. It's okay to say you're scared too. Saying "I don't know either" out loud, together, helped us more than pretending.

## What I wish someone had told me

Not medical tips, just the things I needed to hear when we walked through our front door:

- **Being terrified on the first night doesn't mean something is wrong.** It means you love them and you're paying attention.
- **Relief and fear can arrive together.** You can be desperate to go home and frightened to leave, at the same time.
- **It's okay to lean on your partner completely.** Recovering is part of caring for your baby, not a break from it.
- **You're allowed to ask who to call.** It's not wasting anyone's time.
- **Losing count doesn't make you a bad mother.** It makes you a tired one.
- **Your recovery matters too.** You're a person who just had surgery, not only the baby's mum.

In the hospital I had a call bell. At home, I had a pile of booklets and a phone full of half-finished notes. There had to be a better way to carry everything we needed to know.
